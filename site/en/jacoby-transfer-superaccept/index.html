Superaccepting Jacoby Transfer

Application, examples and responses

After Jacoby Transfer, opener can show 17 HCP and four-card support by superaccepting. Responder then knows of at least a nine-card fit and can reassess whether to stop, bid game or explore slam.

When does it apply?

We assume a balanced 1NT opening with 15–17 HCP, no opposing intervention. In this version every superaccept requires 17 HCP and exactly four cards in responder’s major. Otherwise accept at the two level; five-card support is outside this lesson.

What does it achieve?

A nine-card fit can provide extra tricks. Knowing opener’s doubleton helps responder judge ruffing values in the shorter trump hand and the usefulness of queens and jacks opposite shortness. Consider trump quality and the whole hand.

A superaccept does not force game. The initial Transfer can be made with zero HCP, so we preserve a way to stop at the three level.

How do we superaccept?

Bid the doubleton suit as cheaply as possible. If the doubleton is in the suit of the Transfer bid—diamonds after 2♦ or hearts after 2♥—bid 2NT instead. Superaccepts in a new suit or 2NT are artificial and forcing for one round. Three of the major showing 4333 may be passed.

Points to remember

The initial Transfer promises no points. A new suit by opener shows a doubleton, not length or a control. Here 2NT is artificial, not a natural invitation. A retransfer is not a control-bid. Asking for aces does not replace evaluating tricks, side controls and trumps. Other methods use different meanings; agree the version first.

Sources and the version taught

The doubleton method and 2NT preserving a retransfer follow Byron Lund, How to Play Expert Bridge, pages 82–90. Lund suggests three of the major with 4333 and 18 HCP, downgraded for shape; our lesson uses 17 HCP for 4333 too. Responder’s continuations are our stated agreements, not every extra treatment in the book.

Robert S. Todd supports the retransfer principle; his superaccept ranges and meanings differ. Larry Cohen explains economical first- or second-round control-bidding and when to use Blackwood. The text and example hands were created for Leonardo Bridge.
