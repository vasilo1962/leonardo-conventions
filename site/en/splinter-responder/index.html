Splinter by responder

Application, examples and responses

A Splinter is a double jump in a new suit. It shows at least four-card support for partner, a singleton or void in the suit bid, and game-going strength. This lesson concerns responder. Splinters by opener, after partner's response, will be covered in a separate lesson.

When does it apply?

Partner opens 1♥ or 1♠, promising at least five cards, and the next opponent passes. We have at least four-card support, a singleton or void elsewhere, and enough strength for game. Our version covers first- or second-seat openings, an unpassed responder, and no opposing double or overcall.

What does it achieve?

In bridge, the location of our honors matters as well as the total points. The conventional double jump agrees opener's major as trumps and is game forcing, without committing the partnership to slam.

Partner revalues the hand. Low cards opposite our singleton or void are favorable: partner's strength lies elsewhere. Kings, queens and jacks in that suit may be worth less than their point count suggests. An ace remains useful opposite a singleton; opposite a void its value depends on the whole hand.

How much strength?

We need game values opposite a normal opening, assessing high-card points, support and distribution together. The direct Splinter describes a relatively limited hand; slam becomes attractive when the hands fit well. Exact limits require partnership agreement: there is no universal point range.

With a much stronger hand, prefer Jacoby 2NT to preserve room and learn more about opener. The following Splinter examples have 13 HCP; this is an illustration, not a compulsory or exclusive limit.

Examples

Partner opens and right-hand opponent passes.

1. Spade support and a singleton diamond

After 1♠:

♠ KQ74spades: king, queen, 7, 4.
♥ A863hearts: ace, 8, 6, 3.
♦ 6diamonds: 6.
♣ KJ85clubs: king, jack, 8, 5.

Bid 4♦: 13 HCP, four spades and a singleton diamond. Show game strength and let partner judge how well the honors fit our distribution.

2. Heart support and a singleton spade

After 1♥:

♠ 7spades: 7.
♥ KQ84hearts: king, queen, 8, 4.
♦ A963diamonds: ace, 9, 6, 3.
♣ KJ85clubs: king, jack, 8, 5.

Bid 3♠: 13 HCP and four hearts. The spade bid shows a singleton or void, rather than length, and agrees hearts as trumps.

3. Heart support and a club void

After 1♥:

♠ KJ83spades: king, jack, 8, 3.
♥ AQ74hearts: ace, queen, 7, 4.
♦ K9642diamonds: king, 9, 6, 4, 2.
♣ —clubs: void, no cards.

Bid 4♣: 13 HCP, four hearts and no clubs. The same bid could show a singleton club. This description helps opener assess the partnership's prospects.

4. Strong support without a singleton or void

After 1♠:

♠ AQ74spades: ace, queen, 7, 4.
♥ K85hearts: king, 8, 5.
♦ KJ6diamonds: king, jack, 6.
♣ K83clubs: king, 8, 3.

Bid 2NT Jacoby: 16 HCP and four-card support, but no singleton or void. Wanting to explore slam is not enough reason to make a Splinter.

How do we continue?

The opponents pass throughout. Opener knows that we will play at least game in the agreed major.

We use first- or second-round controls: an ace or void, a king or singleton. When showing a control, bid the cheapest available new suit containing one. This is not a search for another fit. Assess trump quality, useful outside honors and the danger of two quick losers elsewhere; a high point count alone does not justify continuing.

1. Stop in game

Open 1♠; partner bids 4♦ Splinter:

♠ AJ853spades: ace, jack, 8, 5, 3.
♥ K72hearts: king, 7, 2.
♦ KQ4diamonds: king, queen, 4.
♣ T6clubs: ten, 6.

Bid 4♠: 13 HCP, but five are in diamonds opposite partner's singleton or void. The remaining cards do not justify slam exploration. Holding the heart king does not oblige us to show a control.

2. Continue with useful honors

Open 1♠; partner bids 4♦ Splinter:

♠ AKJ85spades: ace, king, jack, 8, 5.
♥ AQ7hearts: ace, queen, 7.
♦ 864diamonds: 8, 6, 4.
♣ A5clubs: ace, 5.

Bid 4♥, showing a heart control and slam interest. All 18 HCP are outside diamonds; we have good trumps and controls in the other two suits. This does not promise heart length or settle the final contract.

3. Show the cheapest control first

Open 1♥; partner bids 3♠ Splinter:

♠ 94spades: 9, 4.
♥ AQJ86hearts: ace, queen, jack, 8, 6.
♦ A7diamonds: ace, 7.
♣ KQ84clubs: king, queen, 8, 4.

Bid 4♣: 16 HCP, good trumps, useful minor-suit honors and no spade points. Show the club king as a second-round control before the diamond ace; do not skip clubs to bid 4♦.

Responder's next bid

If opener bids game, responder passes, even with a maximum within the agreed limits. The hand has already been described. After a control bid, cooperate according to partnership agreements. Ask for aces or key cards once controls and trump quality are sufficiently known. Do not automatically bid 4NT after every Splinter. With a void, simply counting aces may not tell us which are useful.

Points to remember

  • At least four-card support is required. A singleton or void alone is insufficient.
  • The only direct Splinter at the three level here is 1♥ – 3♠, showing spade shortness.
  • Our version avoids a singleton ace or king. Partner could misjudge the other honors in that suit. With game values and four-card support, Jacoby 2NT is an alternative.
  • Agree strength limits; do not describe a much stronger hand as a limited Splinter.
  • Game values do not guarantee slam: honor location and controls matter more than a simple total.
  • Opener's Splinter is a separate application. In 1♣ – 1♥ – 4♦, opener's 4♦ can be a Splinter, with a different strength assessment from responder's version.
  • Opposing interference requires separate agreement. Do not automatically apply these meanings after a double or overcall.
